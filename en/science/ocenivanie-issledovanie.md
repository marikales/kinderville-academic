# Assessment · Thinking and Working Scientifically · Y2–Y6

This page is shared by all classes and all topics.

## Why it's a separate page

Knowing the topic and being able to work scientifically are two different things. A child can tell a fluent story about magnets and still not manage to run an experiment, record the result and compare it with their prediction. And the other way round: measure and record neatly, but never connect it to the topic.

So we assess them separately. **Topic assessment** lives on each class's own page: [Year 3](en/science/ocenivanie-year3.md) · [Year 4](en/science/ocenivanie-year4.md) · [Year 5](en/science/ocenivanie-year5.md) · [Year 6](en/science/ocenivanie-year6.md). **Thinking and Working Scientifically** is assessed against this page, and it's one page for the whole year: not per topic, but about how the child works in general.

**The English science lesson is assessed against this same page.** In English the child works with a topic already covered in Russian — so we're not re-checking understanding of the topic, we're checking how they observe, measure, record, test a prediction and talk about the result. The English words matter, but they are not the grade by themselves: a memorised word list with no doing is not yet science.

## The four levels — they work a little differently here

- **Below.** Can't do it even with a prompt.
- **Working Towards.** Does it partly, or only with a direct prompt at every step.
- **Meeting — the expected standard for the year.** Does what's written in their class's column, with ordinary teacher support.
- **Exceeding.** Does it independently and in an unfamiliar task — or is already working at the next class's level.

In other words, the table below describes the **Meeting** level for each class. Want to give an Exceeding? Look one column to the right: that's where the child is already working.

## What "working scientifically" means, class by class

| Skill | Y2 | Y3 | Y4 | Y5 | Y6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Looking and noticing** | Examines things, finds similarities and differences, sorts objects into groups | Sorts not just by appearance but by the result of a test; uses "same — different — similar" | Uses an identification key: finds what something is by its features | Builds a simple identification key of their own, based on visible differences | Builds a key and uses someone else's; classifies by several features at once |
| **Asking questions** | Asks questions about the world and says who or where could answer them | Tells apart a question you can test with an experiment from a question a book answers | Same + understands that different questions need different ways of testing | Chooses for themselves how to test their own question | Chooses the method and explains why it's the right one |
| **Predicting and testing** | Says what they think will happen | Suggests several possible outcomes | Predicts based on what they already know | Predicts in an unfamiliar situation, drawing on knowledge | Same + judges after the experiment how accurate the prediction turned out to be |
| **Measuring** | Measures with improvised units: paces, hand-spans, cups | Measures with a ruler, scales, a thermometer, and explains why that beats improvised units | Knows that a repeated measurement is more reliable than a single one | Decides for themselves when to repeat, and measures as precisely as the task needs | Same, confidently and without being reminded |
| **Recording** | Labels a picture, fills in a simple ready-made table | Enters results in a table, draws a diagram of the phenomenon | Draws a diagram and chooses what exactly to record | Chooses the form of recording to suit the type of enquiry | Same + records so that a stranger could understand it |
| **Running a fair test** | Not yet — just tries things by following instructions | Knows there are five types of enquiry (research from sources, fair test, observing over time, identifying and classifying, pattern seeking) | Names what we change in the experiment and what has to stay the same | Plans the test themselves: what I change, what I measure, what I keep the same | Plans it and explains why without keeping things the same the comparison isn't fair |
| **Making sense of results** | Tells what happened and whether it matched what they thought | Says whether the prediction came true and describes what they noticed | Describes the pattern in words | Notices a result that doesn't fit the overall picture | Same + suggests how to improve the enquiry and explains the suggestion |
| **Presenting data** | Simple towers of cubes or stickers | Bar chart | Bar chart and dot plot | Line graph | Line graph and scatter graph |
| **Working safely** | Follows instructions, doesn't taste things, tidies up after themselves | Names for themselves what could be dangerous in this task | Same + explains how to avoid it | Thinks the dangers through in advance, at the planning stage | Same + keeps an eye on safety in their group |
| **Using models** | Understands a model is not the thing itself but a representation of it; makes a simple model | Tells a diagram from a picture; draws a diagram themselves | Understands a model doesn't show everything; uses a model for scale | Explains a phenomenon using a model | Explains what exactly the model helps us understand — and where it stops working |

## The bar in the English lesson — personal to each child

In the English lesson we are asking two different questions, and they must not be mixed up.

**What the child did as an investigator** — this bar is the same for the whole class, and it's in the table above. Predicting before the experiment, repeating a measurement, filling in a table properly — these are scientific moves, and they don't depend on how many English words a child has.

**How they talked about it** — this bar is personal, set by their level of English.

How the personal bar works:

- **A gap means a child speaking below their own level.** A beginner who says two words that are to the point has done their job. A strong speaker who gets away with the same two words has not.
- **Falling short of the stronger group is not a gap.** We don't compare children with each other.
- **Silence is always a gap.** If a child has done the work with their hands and said nothing about it, the lesson did not happen — whatever their level.
- **Explaining "why" in English is not off limits — it is expected, at the child's level.** We ask "why" of everyone. It's the answers that differ, not the question.

**The ladder of talk.** Within one lesson children stand on different steps: name → describe → report → explain why. Over the year every child moves up at least one.

**The same idea at three levels.** The question "why does a volcano erupt?":

| Level | What the child says |
|---|---|
| beginner | *"Hot rock comes up. The mountain opens."* |
| developing | *"The volcano erupts because hot rock pushes up."* |
| strong | *"It erupts because pressure builds up under the ground until the rock breaks through."* |

All three are reasoning about cause. Simple words do not mean simple thinking.

**Where the level comes from.** Not from guesswork: at the start of the year, and after every regrouping of the English sets, the science teacher gets a list from the English teachers — who is in the beginner group and who is in the advanced one. Without that list the personal bar turns into a hunch, and the assessment drifts.

**The first weeks of September,** while the diagnostics are still running and the sets don't exist yet: the science teacher sets each child's level provisionally, from what she sees in the lesson. Once the diagnostics are done, the levels are checked against the list, and Module I assessments are revisited where they differ.

More on this in the [Course aims](en/science/README.md), section "Where we look for what".

## What this looks like in the English lesson

In each example the level is given for the **scientific move**, while the talk is shown at three levels: all three count, as long as they match the child's own level.

**Y3, shadows.**
*Science:* traced the shadow, moved the torch, traced again, showed the difference — Meeting. Said "I think it will be longer" before the experiment and then checked — Exceeding.
*Talk:* *"Long. More long."* · *"The shadow is longer now."* · *"The shadow is longer because the torch is lower."*

**Y4, conductors.**
*Science:* built the circuit, tested objects one by one, ticked the table — Meeting. Suggested testing something else and said why — Exceeding.
*Talk:* *"Metal — yes. Plastic — no."* · *"The metal spoon works, the plastic one doesn't."* · *"Metals let the electricity through, so the bulb lights up."*

**Y5, sound.**
*Science:* changed the tension, listened, recorded — Meeting. Repeated the test to make sure — Exceeding.
*Talk:* *"Tight — high. Loose — low."* · *"When it is tighter, the sound is higher."* · *"The tighter the band, the faster it vibrates, so the pitch goes up."*

**What we don't count at any level:** the child correctly repeated ten words after the teacher and did nothing with their hands. Learning the words is good — but that's the outcome of an English language lesson, not a science lesson.
