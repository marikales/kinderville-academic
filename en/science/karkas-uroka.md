# Enquiry Lesson Framework

This is a suggestion, not a required sequence. The framework is for the lessons where we want children to **find something out for themselves**, rather than listen and repeat. Not every lesson needs to be like this — one or two per module is enough. The rest run as usual.

Where to go from problem to solution, and where it's simpler to explain first — that's the teacher's call. Some topics just can't be investigated hands-on: you can't test the structure of the Solar System with your own hands. And with some it would be a real shame to explain first: a child will work out in five minutes what sticks to a magnet — and remember it forever.

---

## How the lesson runs

**1. A question or a problem.** We don't start with the topic — we start with something puzzling. Show an object, a situation, a contradiction — and ask. It's a good sign if the answer isn't obvious and different opinions appear in the class.

**2. What do you think?** Collect predictions. Write every version on the board; don't reject any and don't praise any — just record whose idea is whose. Children need to see that there are several versions and that right now nobody knows which one is true.

**3. Let's find out.** An experiment, an observation, a measurement, looking it up in a book — depends on the question. Children work with their hands, in pairs or small groups. The teacher walks around asking questions, not showing answers.

**4. What did we get?** Gather the results. Go back to the board: whose prediction held up, whose didn't. Crucially: **a wrong prediction is not an embarrassment — it's how science normally works.** A child who made a wrong guess and tested it themselves has done exactly what scientists do.

**5. Name it and explain it.** Only now do we introduce the name of the phenomenon and the explanation. By this point the children have already seen the thing we're naming — the word lands on experience, not on thin air.

**6. Where next.** One sentence: where this shows up in real life, where we'll meet it in the next lesson, what they can now test at home.

Step five can swap places with step three if the topic calls for it. Steps one and two are best not skipped — they're what creates the interest.

## Three examples from Module I

Module I is "Earth and Space" — the one we start the year with.

**Why is it light in the day and dark at night (Y3–Y4).**
The question: "Does the Sun switch off at night?" Collect the versions — you'll usually get "it goes away", "it hides behind the clouds" and "the Earth spins". Test it: a globe or a ball with a little sticker person on it, and a torch as the only light source in a darkened classroom. Turn it slowly and watch when the little person is in the light and when in shadow. Check whose version held up. Name it: the Earth's rotation on its axis, a day. Where next: then why is it already evening in Moscow while it's daytime here?

**Why does the Moon look different every day (Y3–Y6).**
This is an observing-over-time enquiry — it doesn't fit into one lesson, and that's a good thing. Start with the question and the versions, then set up a month-long observation sheet: sketch the Moon every evening. Some children will forget — that's a conversation too, about why scientists keep records regularly. After two or three weeks, look at the row of sketches together and hunt for the pattern. Only then explain it through the lit part of a sphere — the ball-and-torch model makes sense now, because the children have already seen the sequence.

**What is the Earth made of (Y4).**
A provocation: "Nobody has ever been down inside the Earth. So how do they know there's a core?" Children offer their versions — dug down, X-rayed it, guessed. Cut something layered in half (a boiled egg, a pear, a layered plasticine ball they made themselves). Discuss how you can learn about the inside without cutting: by how sound or shaking passes through. Name the layers. Where next: so where does a volcano come from?

## Five types of enquiry

It's worth trying all five over the year, not just experiments. Children should come to see that "investigating" doesn't always mean "mixing things in a cup".

| Type | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Research from sources** | People already know the answer — find it and understand it | What the planets are called, how many there are |
| **Fair test** | Change one thing, keep everything else the same | What rolls further — on the carpet and on the table |
| **Observing over time** | Watch for a long time and keep records | Phases of the Moon over a month, a seed sprouting |
| **Identifying and classifying** | Sort things into groups by their features | Which materials stick to a magnet |
| **Pattern seeking** | Collect lots of cases and look for a rule | The closer the torch, the bigger the shadow — is it always true? |

If a whole year's worth of notebooks contains nothing but fair tests, it's time to mix it up. Observing over time is especially handy because it takes almost no lesson time: two minutes at the start, but it stretches over weeks.

## When the lesson isn't working

**Everyone's silent, no versions.** Usually the question is too abstract, or the children are afraid of being wrong. What helps: offer two versions yourself — "some people think this, others think that — what about you?" — and ask not for an answer, but for a choice. Choosing is easier than inventing.

**Everyone's shouting at once.** Give thirty seconds to think in silence, then discuss in pairs, then out loud. Half the noise disappears into the pairs.

**The experiment failed, the result is wrong.** That's not a failed lesson. Work out together what might have interfered, and, if there's time, try again. A child who has watched an adult stay calm about a failed experiment has learned more about science than from a successful one.

**The children guessed the answer straight away.** So the question was easy — no harm done. Raise the stakes on the spot: "What if we do it the other way round? What if the object is heavier?"

**Ran out of time, stopped halfway.** Better to finish next lesson than cram the explanation into the last two minutes. Children come back to unfinished business well — next lesson you won't even need to warm up their interest.

**It feels like nothing fits into one hour a week.** True — if you make every lesson an enquiry lesson. That's why one or two per module is enough; the rest are ordinary lessons.
